# Domain 1: Philosophy of Mind -- Counter-Arguments

## Thesis Under Audit
**"Consciousness is the fundamental substrate of reality -- not emergent from physical processes."**

This document collects the strongest academic counter-arguments against this thesis for adversarial review purposes. The objective is maximum-strength steelmanning; no attempt is made to defend the thesis.

---

## Counter-Argument 1: The Combination Problem (Subject, Quality, and Structure)

### (a) STEELMAN STATEMENT

The combination problem for panpsychism is not a technical difficulty analogous to the hard problem facing physicalism -- it is deeper. The hard problem asks how experience arises from non-experience; the combination problem asks how experience arises from *experience* -- and still has no answer (Chalmers, 2016/2017; Mendelovici, 2014; James, 1890).

William James identified the core difficulty in 1890: conscious states are "shut in their own skin, windowless, ignorant of what the other feelings are and mean." Take a hundred feelings, pack them as close together as you can -- each remains isolated. James argued that any resultant consciousness would be a "101st feeling," having no intelligible relation to its supposed constituents (James, 1890, p. 160; as cited in Chalmers, 2016).

David Chalmers (2016/2017) systematized the problem into three dimensions:

1. **Subject Combination Problem**: How do micro-subjects combine to yield macro-subjects? Subjectivity seems inherently bounded -- a subject is a perspective defined partly by what it excludes. If electron A has a micro-perspective and electron B has another, what could it mean for those perspectives to *merge*? (Chalmers, 2016, pp. 181-186). Sam Coleman (2014) argued this is demonstrably incoherent: if subjects A and B combine into subject C, either A and B cease to exist (in which case nothing "combined"; something new just appeared) or A, B, and C all coexist, and C is an addition rather than a combination (Coleman, 2014, pp. 25-30).

2. **Quality Combination Problem**: How do micro-qualities combine to yield macro-qualities? There is a vast array of macro-qualities (colors, sounds, emotions), but presumably only a limited palette of micro-qualities corresponding to fundamental microphysical properties. How can a handful of micro-qualities combine to yield the vast array of macro-qualities? This is the "palette problem" (Chalmers, 2016, pp. 190-193).

3. **Structure Combination Problem**: How does microphenomenal structure yield macrophenomenal structure? Phenomenal states have rich spatial, temporal, and intentional structure. How does the structure of micro-experiences generate the structure of macro-experience? (Chalmers, 2016, pp. 193-195).

Philip Goff (2009) strengthened the problem by arguing that panpsychism "doesn't help us explain consciousness" at all -- it simply relocates the explanatory gap from the physical-to-phenomenal level to the micro-phenomenal-to-macro-phenomenal level. The conceivability of "micro-experiential zombies" (beings physically identical to us whose micro-parts each have experience but where no macro-experience exists) mirrors the original zombie argument against physicalism: micro-facts do not seem to necessitate macro-consciousness (Goff, 2009, pp. 295-300; Goff, 2017, pp. 181-186).

The phenomenal bonding solution proposed by Goff (2017) and endorsed by Miller (2018) postulates a primitive relation that binds micro-subjects into macro-subjects. But Chalmers (2016, pp. 200-204) notes that we lack any positive concept of what this relation could be -- the proposal risks being empty. Moreover, the boundary problem (Rosenberg, 2004; Dainton, 2011) threatens: whatever mechanism solves the combination problem would also fuse the entire cosmos into one subject, annihilating individual subjects like us (Chalmers, 2016, pp. 195-198).

### (c) SEVERITY

**Show-stopper.** The combination problem strikes at the very mechanism by which consciousness-as-fundamental is supposed to explain macro-level experience. If micro-experiences cannot intelligibly combine into macro-experiences, then positing consciousness at the fundamental level provides no explanatory advance over dualism. As Chalmers (2016, p. 181) notes, the problem "has no widely accepted solution." The problem is not merely unsolved; there are principled reasons to doubt it *can* be solved, given that subjects seem definitionally bounded and perspectival.

### (d) WHAT WOULD ADDRESS IT

A positive, conceptually transparent account of the "phenomenal bonding" relation that (i) allows micro-subjects to compose macro-subjects, (ii) does not overgenerate and fuse the entire cosmos into one subject (solving the boundary problem), and (iii) has independent motivation beyond ad hoc postulation. Roelofs (2019) offers the most developed attempt; if his "experience-sharing" framework could be made independently plausible and shown to avoid Coleman's (2014) incoherence arguments, this would significantly blunt the objection. Alternatively, a defensible version of cosmopsychism (Shani, 2015) that derives macro-subjects from one cosmic subject without decomposition could work, but faces its own "decombination problem."

### (e) WHAT WOULD NOT

- Appealing to "emergence" without explaining *how* emergence works in the phenomenal case; this simply reintroduces the hard problem at a different level.
- Claiming that combination is "just a brute fact" -- this abandons the very explanatory promise that makes panpsychism attractive over dualism.
- Appealing to quantum holism or entanglement as analogies; these explain physical correlation, not phenomenal unity or subject-merger.
- Dismissing the problem as merely "epistemic" (our inability to *know* how combination works); the issue is conceptual, not merely epistemic -- we lack the conceptual resources to make sense of subject-merger.

## Counter-Argument 2: The Relocation Objection -- Consciousness-as-Fundamental Relocates Rather Than Solves the Hard Problem

### (a) STEELMAN STATEMENT

Positing consciousness as the fundamental substrate of reality does not *solve* the hard problem of consciousness; it merely *relocates* it. The original hard problem asks: why does consciousness arise from non-conscious physical processes? The consciousness-as-fundamental thesis replaces this with an equally mysterious question: why does *macro* consciousness arise from *micro* consciousness? (Goff, 2009; Chalmers, 2016; Aleksiev, 2021).

Philip Goff (2009) made this point forcefully: "No matter what weird and wacky conscious states we attribute to your micro-level bits, it seems conceivable that those micro subjects exist in the absence of some further subject at the macro-level" (Goff, 2009, p. 300; cf. Goff, 2017, p. 182). This "micro-experiential zombie" argument shows that the explanatory gap between the fundamental and the derived replicates the original gap between the physical and the phenomenal. The same conceivability arguments that motivate panpsychism against physicalism -- zombie arguments, knowledge arguments, explanatory gap arguments -- can be redeployed *against* panpsychism at the level of combination (Chalmers, 2016, pp. 195-198; Goff, 2017, pp. 181-186).

Moreover, panpsychism faces a *reverse* explanatory gap that physicalism does not: the "missing objects problem" or "missing entities problem" (Aleksiev, 2021; Howell, 2015; Pereboom, 2011; Chalmers, 2020). Physicalism has trouble explaining consciousness from physical structure; panpsychism has trouble explaining *physical structure* from consciousness. Specifically:

- **Spacetime Gap**: Human experiences do not seem to have the metric structure of spacetime. If fundamental experiences are modeled on human experiences (the "Good Model" thesis that panpsychists need to motivate the combination problem at all), then fundamental experiences lack the metric structure of spacetime. There is an explanatory gap between experiences (as grounds) and spacetime (as groundee) (Aleksiev, 2021, pp. 194-210).
- **Quantum State Gap**: The high-dimensional quantum state (3N-dimensional configuration space) has no analogue in experience. If fundamental experiences cannot ground this physical structure, panpsychism fails to reconstruct the physical world (Aleksiev, 2021, pp. 210-214).
- **Quantum Gravity Gap**: Timeless quantum-gravitational entities (e.g., spin networks in loop quantum gravity) lack temporal structure. Human experiences are essentially temporal. Via the Good Model thesis, fundamental experiences are temporal too. But then they cannot ground timeless physical entities (Aleksiev, 2021, pp. 214-218).

As Aleksiev (2021, pp. 218-222) concludes: "Panpsychism opens more problems than it closes... Panpsychists are obliged to solve all instances of the missing entities problem; otherwise, panpsychism cannot be considered a viable theory of consciousness."

The relocation objection thus operates on two fronts: (1) the hard problem is not solved but pushed down to the level of combination, and (2) panpsychism creates *new* explanatory gaps that physicalism does not face -- gaps between consciousness and the *physical structure* of the world.

### (c) SEVERITY

**Show-stopper.** If the relocation objection holds, consciousness-as-fundamental is not merely incomplete; it fails to deliver on its central explanatory promise. The thesis is motivated precisely by the claim that it *solves* the hard problem where physicalism cannot. If it merely relocates the problem while adding new gaps of its own, it is a net theoretical loss. The missing entities problem is particularly damaging because it targets the panpsychist's ability to account for the physical world at all.

### (d) WHAT WOULD ADDRESS IT

Two developments would significantly blunt this objection: (1) a fully worked-out account of how fundamental experiences ground spacetime, the quantum state, and other physical structures -- what Aleksiev (2021) calls "closing the missing entities problem" -- perhaps via a sophisticated functionalist or structuralist story about how experiential properties realize physical-relational properties; and (2) a compelling argument that the combination problem *can* be solved without merely pushing the mystery down a level, perhaps through a non-compositional form of panpsychism (e.g., cosmopsychism) that avoids combination altogether. Goff's (2017, Ch. 9) "phenomenal bonding" proposal or Shani's (2015) cosmopsychism represent the most promising directions.

### (e) WHAT WOULD NOT

- Asserting that the combination problem is "the same kind of problem" as other scientific problems without showing *how* it is solvable; this merely changes the label.
- Claiming that we just need "more science" to understand how experiences combine; the problem is conceptual, not empirical.
- Appealing to "neutral monism" without specifying the nature of the neutral entities; this merely introduces a third mystery.
- Arguing that physicalism also has problems; this is true but irrelevant -- the question is whether panpsychism does *better*, not whether physicalism is perfect.

## Counter-Argument 3: Illusionism and the Meta-Problem

### (a) STEELMAN STATEMENT

Illusionism, defended systematically by Keith Frankish (2016) and endorsed by Daniel Dennett (1991, 2016), holds that phenomenal consciousness -- the "what it's like" aspect of experience -- does not exist. Rather, our sense that we have experiences with special qualitative properties is an introspective misrepresentation. There are no phenomenal properties, only representations *as if* there were phenomenal properties (Frankish, 2016, pp. 9-15; Dennett, 1991, Ch. 11; Dennett, 2016).

If illusionism is even *possibly* correct, the entire motivation for positing consciousness as the fundamental substrate of reality collapses. The consciousness-as-fundamental thesis treats phenomenal consciousness as a basic datum requiring fundamental ontological positing. But if phenomenal consciousness is an illusion -- a misrepresentation produced by introspective systems -- then there is no datum to explain, and certainly no reason to build it into the foundations of reality (Frankish, 2016, pp. 15-20; Dennett, 2016).

David Chalmers (2018) sharpened the challenge by formulating the "meta-problem of consciousness": the problem of explaining why we *think* there is a problem of consciousness. Chalmers showed that if a topic-neutral explanation of our problem intuitions can be given -- an explanation that does not invoke phenomenal consciousness itself -- then a "debunking argument" for illusionism follows: our intuitions about consciousness would be explained independently of consciousness, undermining their evidential value (Chalmers, 2018, pp. 6-15; Chalmers, 2020).

Chalmers (2018) distinguishes six stances on the meta-problem. The last three -- strong illusionism (consciousness does not exist), and two forms of weak illusionism (consciousness exists but is not as we intuit it) -- all require rejecting central intuitions about consciousness. Crucially, Chalmers argues that *only strong illusionism* can genuinely dissolve the hard problem. Weak illusionism, like physicalism, still faces the hard problem. But strong illusionism dissolves it entirely by denying the existence of the explanandum (Chalmers, 2018, pp. 55-58).

The dialectical situation is stark. Consciousness-as-fundamental is committed to the reality of phenomenal consciousness. But if the illusionist can provide a satisfying explanation of why it *seems* to us that we are conscious -- the "illusion problem" -- without positing phenomenal properties, then the consciousness-as-fundamental thesis multiplies entities beyond necessity. As Dennett (2016) puts it, illusionism is "the obvious default theory of consciousness" -- not a radical departure but a conservative physicalist position that should be the starting point. Frankish (2019) adds that if consciousness realists cannot explain how they have "robust direct access" to consciousness, the "hard meta-problem" undermines realism from within.

The burden on consciousness-as-fundamental is therefore twofold: it must (i) decisively rule out illusionism, and (ii) explain why the meta-problem has a solution that *requires* the reality of consciousness (realizationism) rather than one that works independently of it. Neither burden has been discharged.

- Frankish, K. (2016). "Illusionism as a Theory of Consciousness." *Journal of Consciousness Studies*, 23(11-12), 9-39. [Also in K. Frankish (Ed.), *Illusionism*, Andrews UK Limited, 2017, pp. 9-39.]
- Frankish, K. (2019). "The Meta-Problem is the Problem of Consciousness." *Journal of Consciousness Studies*, 26(9-10), 83-94.
- Dennett, D.C. (1991). *Consciousness Explained*. Little, Brown and Company, Ch. 11.
- Dennett, D.C. (2016). "Illusionism as the Obvious Default Theory of Consciousness." Commentary on Chalmers, *Journal of Consciousness Studies*, 23(11-12).
- Chalmers, D.J. (2018). "The Meta-Problem of Consciousness." *Journal of Consciousness Studies*, 25(9-10), 6-61.
- Chalmers, D.J. (2020). "Debunking Arguments for Illusionism about Consciousness." In *Debates on the Meta-Problem of Consciousness*, Imprint Academic.
- Graziano, M.S.A. (2013). *Consciousness and the Social Brain*. Oxford University Press. [Attention Schema Theory as an alternative physicalist account.]

### (c) SEVERITY

**Major.** Illusionism does not directly refute consciousness-as-fundamental; it provides an alternative explanatory framework that, if successful, renders consciousness-as-fundamental explanatorily superfluous. The meta-problem framework sharpens the challenge: if a topic-neutral explanation of consciousness intuitions is forthcoming, the default epistemic position shifts toward illusionism. Chalmers (2018) himself, the leading defender of consciousness realism, concedes that illusionism has been "under-explored" and that he has "more sympathy with it than with most materialist views." However, Chalmers' "Moorean" argument ("people sometimes feel pain") creates a dialectical stalemate, not a refutation.

### (d) WHAT WOULD ADDRESS IT

A successful "realizationist" solution to the meta-problem, showing that consciousness is *crucial* for realizing the processes that generate our judgments about consciousness, so that the truth of those judgments is not a "mere coincidence" (Chalmers, 2018, pp. 40-50). Alternatively, a decisive argument that no topic-neutral explanation of our problem intuitions is possible -- meta-problem nihilism. A further possibility: demonstrating that phenomenal concepts are *referentially transparent* in a way that makes illusionism conceptually incoherent (as Goff, 2017, and others have attempted).

### (e) WHAT WOULD NOT

- Simply asserting "but I know I am conscious" -- the illusionist has a ready explanation of why this judgment seems compelling even if false (introspective misrepresentation).
- Appealing to the "incredibility" of illusionism; Chalmers (2018) explicitly warns that incredulity is not an argument.
- Dismissing illusionism as self-refuting because illusionists must be "conscious of" their own argument; the illusionist accepts the reality of cognitive/access processes, only denies phenomenal properties.
- Claiming that illusionism leaves the "illusion problem" just as hard as the hard problem; Chalmers (2018) agrees but notes that for the illusionist, the illusion problem is the *only* problem.

## Counter-Argument 4: The Epistemic View / Ignorance Hypothesis (Stoljar)

### (a) STEELMAN STATEMENT

Daniel Stoljar's epistemic view (Stoljar, 2006, 2009) offers a powerful alternative explanation of why consciousness seems problematic without either (i) positing consciousness as fundamental or (ii) denying the existence of consciousness. The view has two parts:

1. **The Ignorance Hypothesis (IH)**: We are currently ignorant of a type of non-experiential (physical) fact that is relevant to the nature of consciousness. Science describes the *structure* of the physical world -- spatial, temporal, and causal relations -- but does not reveal the *intrinsic* or *categorical* nature of the entities that bear these relations. The facts about the intrinsic nature of physical entities are the consciousness-relevant facts of which we are ignorant (Stoljar, 2006, Ch. 1-3; Stoljar, 2009, pp. 171-198).

2. **The Dialectical Thesis**: If the ignorance hypothesis is true, the key arguments motivating consciousness-as-fundamental -- the conceivability of zombies, Mary's room, the explanatory gap -- are unpersuasive. These arguments all depend on claims about "all physical facts." If the ignorance hypothesis is true, we have two choices: either "all physical facts" includes the facts of which we are ignorant (in which case zombies are not really conceivable) or it does not (in which case the argument shows only that psychological facts come apart from *some* physical facts, not all) (Stoljar, 2006, Ch. 4-6; Stoljar, 2014 interview).

Stoljar's position is that our puzzlement about consciousness stems from our *mistaken conviction* that facts about conscious experience are not metaphysically determined by non-experiential facts. This conviction arises not because consciousness is actually fundamental, but because we are ignorant of the relevant non-experiential facts. If we knew those facts, consciousness would no longer seem problematic (Stoljar, 2006, pp. 10-15).

Stoljar offers a fable to illustrate: imagine intelligent slugs who live on a mosaic of triangular and pie-slice shaped tiles. The slugs can detect triangles and circles but not pie-slices. They rightly believe that circular facts are determined by non-circular facts. But because they cannot detect pie-slices, they are convinced that triangular facts cannot determine circular facts. Their puzzlement arises from ignorance, not from any metaphysical gap (Stoljar, 2006, pp. 108-110; as reviewed in NDPR, 2007).

The epistemic view has several virtues that make it a serious rival to consciousness-as-fundamental:
- It avoids dualism without positing consciousness as fundamental.
- It is compatible with physical causal closure.
- It does not face the combination problem (since it does not posit micro-experiences).
- It does not face the missing objects problem (since it does not claim the physical world is grounded in experience).
- It has historical precedent: Descartes' argument that no physical being could use language intelligently was undermined by our discovery of computation; C.D. Broad's argument that chemical facts must be metaphysically distinct from physical facts was undermined by quantum chemistry (Stoljar, 2006, pp. 142-155).

Gertler (2009) raises a serious internal challenge: the ignorance Stoljar identifies may threaten physicalism as much as it threatens dualism. But Stoljar (2006, Ch. 8) responds that his view is not a defense of standard physicalism but a reconception of it -- "extended physicalism" that acknowledges our ignorance of the intrinsic physical properties.

- Montero, B. (1999). "The Body Problem." *Nous*, 33(2), 183-200. [On the coherence of the notion of the physical.]

### (c) SEVERITY

**Major.** The epistemic view provides a systematic explanation of why consciousness seems fundamental without actually being so. It deflates the central *motivation* for consciousness-as-fundamental by explaining our problem intuitions as products of ignorance rather than as evidence of metaphysical reality. The view has been influential and has not been decisively refuted. Chalmers (2018, pp. 56-57) classifies it as steering between "Scylla" (non-reductive realism) and "Charybdis" (denial of phenomenal realism), though he ultimately rejects it as insufficient. The primary weakness is that the ignorance hypothesis may seem ad hoc unless supplemented with a positive account of what the missing facts are.

### (d) WHAT WOULD ADDRESS IT

A compelling argument that no amount of additional physical knowledge could close the explanatory gap -- that the gap is *metaphysical*, not *epistemic*. This would require showing that the structure of phenomenal concepts (first-person, perspectival, indexical) is such that no third-personal, objective, non-perspectival facts could render the absence of phenomenal properties inconceivable (Stoljar, 2006, pp. 155-165, considers and responds to this challenge). Alternatively, providing independent evidence for *which* intrinsic physical properties are consciousness-relevant and showing that they are *already experiential* would collapse the epistemic view into a form of panpsychism.

### (e) WHAT WOULD NOT

- Claiming that "we already know all the physical facts" -- this begs the question against the ignorance hypothesis.
- Asserting that phenomenal knowledge is "sui generis" without explaining why this entails metaphysical distinctness.
- Appealing to the "obviousness" of consciousness as fundamental; Stoljar's whole point is that obviousness is a product of ignorance.
- Dismissing the view as "mysterian" -- Stoljar explicitly rejects McGinn's (1989) mysterianism; his ignorance is remediable, not permanent.

## Counter-Argument 5: Critiques of Modal Arguments (Conceivability-Possibility Inference)

### (a) STEELMAN STATEMENT

The consciousness-as-fundamental thesis typically relies on modal arguments: zombie arguments (Chalmers, 1996, 2010), knowledge arguments (Jackson, 1982, 1986), and explanatory gap arguments (Levine, 2001). These arguments share a common structure: they infer from the conceivability of a physical-mental dissociation (e.g., a physical duplicate without consciousness) to the metaphysical possibility of that dissociation, and from there to the falsity of physicalism and the fundamentality of consciousness. But this inference has been subjected to powerful critiques that, if sound, undermine the primary motivation for consciousness-as-fundamental.

**Critique 1: The conceivability-possibility link fails.** Ned Block and Robert Stalnaker (1999) argued that conceivability arguments depend on an undefended assumption about the relation between conceivability and possibility. The existence of a posteriori necessities (Kripke, 1972) shows that conceivability does not entail possibility in general. Block and Stalnaker (1999, pp. 1-5) argued that the standard conceivability arguments against physicalism "depend on a certain fragile and dubious conception of the relation between conceivability and possibility" and that there is no non-question-begging argument for the premise that ideal conceivability entails possibility (Block & Stalnaker, 1999, pp. 15-25; see also Yablo, 1993; Soames, 2004).

**Critique 2: The zombie argument is self-undermining.** Katalin Balog (1999) offered the "Zombie Refutation": if the zombie argument were sound, there would be a possible zombie world in which Zombie-Chalmers gives a zombie-version of the zombie argument, concluding that physicalism is false. But physicalism is *true* in the zombie world (by definition -- it's a physical duplicate of our world). So Zombie-Chalmers' conclusion is false. Since Zombie-Chalmers' premises are true and his reasoning valid, the fault must lie in the conceivability-possibility link (Balog, 1999, pp. 5-10; cf. Chalmers' response in 2004).

**Critique 3: The two-dimensional framework is problematic.** The zombie argument requires the two-dimensional semantic framework: primary and secondary intensions must coincide for both physical and phenomenal concepts. But it is far from clear that they do. If phenomenal concepts have secondary intensions that refer to brain states, then zombies are not 2-possible even if they are 1-conceivable (Cleeveley, 2018; Giberman, 2015). Panpsychism itself complicates the picture: if fundamental physical reality is mental, then the zombie scenario's physical description may already include mental properties, making zombies trivially impossible (Chalmers, 2010, p. 149; Cleeveley, 2018, pp. 2-5).

**Critique 4: A posteriori physicalism is a live option.** Type-B physicalists (Papineau, 2002; Block, 2007) hold that the physical facts metaphysically necessitate phenomenal facts even though there is no a priori entailment. This position is not refuted by conceivability arguments, because it rejects the premise that conceivability entails possibility. The consciousness-as-fundamental thesis must therefore show not just that zombies are conceivable, but that a posteriori physicalism is *false* -- a burden that has not been discharged.

**Critique 5: The panpsychist zombie argument.** Most devastatingly, the very conceivability arguments that motivate consciousness-as-fundamental can be turned *against* it. Goff (2009, 2017) argued that "micro-experiential zombies" -- beings physically identical to us whose micro-parts each have experience but where no macro-experience exists -- are conceivable. If conceivable (and if conceivability entails possibility), then panpsychism is false. Panpsychists must either reject the conceivability-possibility inference (undermining their own argument against physicalism) or accept that their view is refuted by its own methodological commitments (Chalmers, 2016, pp. 195-198; Goff, 2017, pp. 181-186).

### (c) SEVERITY

**Major.** The modal arguments (zombie argument, knowledge argument) are the primary *motivation* for consciousness-as-fundamental. If these arguments are unsound -- or if the conceivability-possibility inference they rely on is unreliable -- then the central case for the thesis collapses. The panpsychist zombie argument is particularly damaging: it threatens to make the position self-refuting by its own methodological commitments. However, this counter-argument does not establish that consciousness-as-fundamental is *false*; it only undermines the standard *arguments for* it. A defender of the thesis could fall back on non-modal arguments (e.g., direct phenomenological evidence, explanatory virtues), though these are comparatively less developed.

### (d) WHAT WOULD ADDRESS IT

A successful defense of modal rationalism (the thesis that ideal conceivability entails possibility) that survives Block-Stalnaker critiques. Chalmers (2002, 2004, 2010) has offered the most developed defense; if his two-dimensional framework can be vindicated against Block-Stalnaker and Balog objections, the zombie argument would be rehabilitated. Alternatively, the consciousness-as-fundamental thesis could be reformulated so as not to depend on modal arguments at all -- e.g., as an inference to the best explanation from the phenomenology of consciousness itself, or from the failure of all physicalist explanatory projects.

### (e) WHAT WOULD NOT

- Simply insisting that zombies "seem possible" -- Block-Stalnaker (1999) showed that this is compatible with physicalism if a posteriori necessities are accepted.
- Appealing to "intuition" without defending the conceivability-possibility link -- this is precisely what the critiques target.
- Rejecting modal rationalism only when it threatens panpsychism while accepting it against physicalism -- this is dialectically inconsistent.
- Claiming that the panpsychist zombie argument "misses the point" -- it uses the very same methodological tools that motivate panpsychism.

## Researcher Notes

1. **The combination problem** remains the most widely acknowledged and severe challenge to panpsychism/consciousness-as-fundamental in mainstream academic philosophy. Chalmers (2016, p. 181) himself describes it as having "no widely accepted solution." This is not a peripheral objection but the central open problem in the field.

2. **The missing entities problem** (Aleksiev, 2021) is newer and less discussed, but potentially equally severe. It has not yet received the sustained attention that the combination problem has. Its status should be flagged as "emerging challenge, not yet fully vetted by the field."

3. **Illusionism** has grown significantly in prominence since Chalmers' (2018) meta-problem framing. Frankish (2016) and Dennett (2016) represent the strongest systematic versions. The dialectical situation is genuinely unsettled.

4. **Stoljar's epistemic view** represents the most sophisticated form of "Russellian physicalism" -- it accepts that physics describes structure but not intrinsic nature, without positing that intrinsic nature is experiential. It is a direct competitor to consciousness-as-fundamental for the same explanatory territory.

5. **Modal argument critiques** from Block-Stalnaker (1999) are widely accepted in the philosophy of mind community. Even many physicalists accept the critiques while rejecting the anti-physicalist conclusions. The consciousness-as-fundamental thesis cannot simply assume that zombie arguments are sound.
