# 1. Philosophy of Mind

## 1.1 The Combination Problem

The combination problem asks how micro-experiences at the fundamental level yield macro-experience. Where the hard problem asks how experience arises from non-experience, the combination problem asks how experience arises from *experience* and finds no satisfactory answer (Chalmers, 2016, pp. 179-181; James, 1890, p. 160).

### 1.1.1 Subject Combination Problem

**(a) STEELMAN STATEMENT.** William James observed that conscious states are "shut in their own skin, windowless, ignorant of what the other feelings are and mean" (James, 1890, p. 160). Pack a hundred feelings together and each remains isolated; any resultant is merely a "101st feeling" with no intelligible relation to its supposed constituents. Sam Coleman (2014) formalized the incoherence: if subjects A and B combine into C, either A and B cease to exist — nothing genuinely combined — or all three coexist, and C is an addition rather than a combination (Coleman, 2014, pp. 25-30). A subject is a perspective defined by what it excludes; no known relation makes sense of subject-merger. As Chalmers (2016, p. 181) notes, the problem "has no widely accepted solution."

**(b) PRIMARY SOURCE(S).** James, W. (1890). *The Principles of Psychology*, Vol. 1, Ch. 6. Henry Holt, p. 160. Coleman, S. (2014). "The Real Combination Problem." *Erkenntnis*, 79, 19-44. Chalmers, D.J. (2016). "The Combination Problem for Panpsychism." In *Panpsychism: Contemporary Perspectives*, Oxford University Press, pp. 181-186. Roelofs, L. (2019). *Combining Minds*. Oxford University Press.

**(c) SEVERITY.** **Show-stopper.** If subjects cannot combine, the mechanism by which consciousness-as-fundamental delivers its explanandum is blocked at the first step.

**(d) WHAT WOULD ADDRESS IT.** A conceptually transparent account of "phenomenal bonding" that composes micro-subjects into macro-subjects without cosmic overgeneration, with independent motivation. Roelofs (2019) offers the most developed attempt.

**(e) WHAT WOULD NOT.** Appeals to "emergence" without explaining how it works in the phenomenal case; claiming combination is a "brute fact"; quantum holism or entanglement analogies — these explain physical correlation, not phenomenal unity; dismissing the problem as merely "epistemic" when the obstacle is conceptual.

### 1.1.2 Quality, Structure, and Bonding Problems

**(a) STEELMAN STATEMENT.** The *quality combination problem*: no compositional principle has been shown to generate the vast landscape of macro-qualities from a limited palette of micro-qualities (Chalmers, 2016, pp. 190-193). The *structure combination problem*: it is not obvious that point-like particles bear any compositional relation to the spatial and temporal structure of macro-experience (Chalmers, 2016, pp. 193-195). The *phenomenal bonding* solution postulates a primitive relation binding micro-subjects (Goff, 2017; Miller, 2018), but Chalmers (2016, pp. 200-204) notes this risks emptiness — we lack any positive concept of what this relation is. The *boundary problem* (Rosenberg, 2004; Dainton, 2011) threatens: whatever solves combination, if sufficiently general, fuses the cosmos into one subject.

**(b) PRIMARY SOURCE(S).** Chalmers, D.J. (2016). "The Combination Problem for Panpsychism," pp. 190-204. Goff, P. (2017). *Consciousness and Fundamental Reality*, Ch. 8-9. Rosenberg, G. (2004). *A Place for Consciousness*. Oxford University Press. Miller, G. (2018). "Can Subjects Be Proper Parts of Subjects?" *Ratio*, 31(2), 137-154.

**(c) SEVERITY.** **Show-stopper.** The combination of palette opacity, structural gap, emptiness risk, and boundary overgeneration means the leading proposed solution may not be a solution at all.

**(d) WHAT WOULD ADDRESS IT.** An independently motivated, positively characterized bonding relation with a principled account of why it binds at the level of brains but not solar systems, together with a compositional principle for experiential qualities and structures.

**(e) WHAT WOULD NOT.** Treating phenomenal bonding as an unquestioned primitive; color-mixing or chord-harmonic analogies — these are functional processes, not phenomenal quality emergence; asserting "complexity yields novelty" without specifying the mechanism.

## 1.2 The Relocation Objection and Missing Entities Problem

### 1.2.1 Consciousness-as-Fundamental Relocates Rather Than Solves the Hard Problem

**(a) STEELMAN STATEMENT.** Positing consciousness as fundamental does not solve the hard problem; it relocates it. The thesis replaces the question of why consciousness arises from non-conscious processes with an equally mysterious one: why does *macro* consciousness arise from *micro* consciousness? (Goff, 2009; Chalmers, 2016). Goff (2009, p. 300) formulated the "micro-experiential zombie" argument: it seems conceivable that micro-subjects exist without any macro-level subject. The same conceivability arguments used against physicalism redeploy *against* panpsychism at the level of combination (Goff, 2009, pp. 295-300; Goff, 2017, pp. 181-186). The explanatory gap replicates at a lower level.

**(b) PRIMARY SOURCE(S).** Goff, P. (2009). "Why Panpsychism Doesn't Help Us Explain Consciousness." *Dialectica*, 63(3), 289-311. Goff, P. (2017). *Consciousness and Fundamental Reality*, pp. 181-186. Chalmers, D.J. (2016). "The Combination Problem for Panpsychism," pp. 195-198.

**(c) SEVERITY.** **Show-stopper.** The thesis is motivated by the claim that it *solves* the hard problem where physicalism cannot. If it merely relocates the problem while adding new gaps, it is a net theoretical failure.

**(d) WHAT WOULD ADDRESS IT.** A non-compositional form of panpsychism, such as cosmopsychism (Shani, 2015), that avoids the micro-to-macro gap by deriving individual subjects from a cosmic subject without passing through combination.

**(e) WHAT WOULD NOT.** Asserting the combination problem is "the same kind of problem" as other scientific problems without showing how it is solvable; claiming "more science" will resolve it when the obstacle is conceptual.

### 1.2.2 The Missing Entities Problem

**(a) STEELMAN STATEMENT.** Panpsychism faces a reverse explanatory gap: the "missing entities problem" (Aleksiev, 2021). Physicalism struggles to explain consciousness from physical structure; panpsychism struggles to explain *physical structure* from consciousness. Aleksiev (2021) identified three gaps. The **spacetime gap**: human experiences lack the metric structure of spacetime, and via the "Good Model" thesis, fundamental experiences cannot ground it (pp. 194-210). The **quantum state gap**: the 3N-dimensional quantum configuration space has no analogue in experience (pp. 210-214). The **quantum gravity gap**: timeless entities such as spin networks lack temporal structure, but experience is essentially temporal (pp. 214-218). Aleksiev: "Panpsychism opens more problems than it closes" (pp. 218-222).

**(b) PRIMARY SOURCE(S).** Aleksiev, D. (2021). "Missing Entities: Has Panpsychism Lost the Physical World?" *Journal of Consciousness Studies*, 28(9-10), 194-222. Howell, R. (2015). "The Location of Consciousness." In *Consciousness in the Physical World*, Oxford University Press. Pereboom, D. (2011). *Consciousness and the Prospects of Physicalism*, p. 115.

**(c) SEVERITY.** **Show-stopper.** A theory that cannot account for spacetime, quantum states, and quantum-gravitational entities provides a theory of consciousness at the cost of losing physics.

**(d) WHAT WOULD ADDRESS IT.** A worked-out account of how fundamental experiences ground physical structure — perhaps via a structuralist story about how experiential properties realize physical-relational properties.

**(e) WHAT WOULD NOT.** Appealing to "neutral monism" without specifying the neutral entities; arguing that physicalism also has problems — true but irrelevant to whether panpsychism does *better*.

### 1.2.3 The Reversal of Explanatory Burden

Physicalism faces one hard problem. Consciousness-as-fundamental faces the combination problem, relocation, three missing entities gaps, and the boundary problem — at least five explanatory gaps while closing none. If correct, the thesis is a net theoretical loss (Aleksiev, 2021, pp. 218-222; Goff, 2009).

## 1.3 Illusionism and the Meta-Problem

### 1.3.1 Frankish and Dennett's Illusionism

**(a) STEELMAN STATEMENT.** Illusionism, defended by Frankish (2016) and Dennett (1991, 2016), holds that phenomenal consciousness does not exist. Our sense that we have qualitative experiences is an introspective misrepresentation — there are only representations *as if* there were phenomenal properties (Frankish, 2016, pp. 9-15; Dennett, 1991, Ch. 11). If illusionism is possibly correct, the motivation for consciousness-as-fundamental collapses: the thesis treats phenomenal consciousness as a datum requiring ontological grounding, but if it is an illusion, there is no datum to explain (Frankish, 2016, pp. 15-20). Dennett (2016) calls illusionism "the obvious default theory of consciousness."

**(b) PRIMARY SOURCE(S).** Frankish, K. (2016). "Illusionism as a Theory of Consciousness." *Journal of Consciousness Studies*, 23(11-12), 9-39. Dennett, D.C. (1991). *Consciousness Explained*, Ch. 11. Dennett, D.C. (2016). "Illusionism as the Obvious Default Theory of Consciousness." *Journal of Consciousness Studies*, 23(11-12).

**(c) SEVERITY.** **Major.** Illusionism does not directly refute the thesis; it provides an alternative framework that, if successful, renders consciousness-as-fundamental explanatorily superfluous.

**(d) WHAT WOULD ADDRESS IT.** An argument that phenomenal concepts are *referentially transparent* in a way making illusionism conceptually incoherent — that one cannot represent *as if* pain without something it is like to be in pain. Goff (2017) has attempted this.

**(e) WHAT WOULD NOT.** Asserting "but I know I am conscious" — the illusionist explains why this judgment seems compelling even if false; appealing to the "incredibility" of illusionism — Chalmers (2018) explicitly warns this is not an argument.

### 1.3.2 Chalmers' Meta-Problem and the Burden on Consciousness-as-Fundamental

**(a) STEELMAN STATEMENT.** Chalmers (2018) formulated the "meta-problem of consciousness": explaining why we *think* there is a hard problem. If a topic-neutral explanation of consciousness intuitions can be given, a debunking argument follows — our intuitions explained independently of consciousness undermines their evidential value (Chalmers, 2018, pp. 6-15). Chalmers argues that *only strong illusionism* genuinely dissolves the hard problem; strong illusionism denies the existence of the explanandum entirely (Chalmers, 2018, pp. 55-58). The thesis must simultaneously (i) rule out illusionism, (ii) explain why the meta-problem requires consciousness's reality, and (iii) vindicate direct epistemic access. Frankish (2019) adds that if realists cannot explain their "robust direct access," the "hard meta-problem" undermines realism from within. None of these three burdens has been discharged.

**(b) PRIMARY SOURCE(S).** Chalmers, D.J. (2018). "The Meta-Problem of Consciousness." *Journal of Consciousness Studies*, 25(9-10), 6-61. Frankish, K. (2019). "The Meta-Problem is the Problem of Consciousness." *Journal of Consciousness Studies*, 26(9-10), 83-94. Chalmers, D.J. (2020). "Debunking Arguments for Illusionism." In *Debates on the Meta-Problem of Consciousness*, Imprint Academic.

**(c) SEVERITY.** **Major.** The meta-problem makes the illusionist challenge more rigorous by translating it into an explanatory demand. If a topic-neutral explanation of consciousness intuitions is forthcoming, the default epistemic position shifts toward illusionism. This is a cumulative burden: three independent tasks, none accomplished.

**(d) WHAT WOULD ADDRESS IT.** A "realizationist" solution showing consciousness is *crucial* for realizing the processes generating our judgments about it (Chalmers, 2018, pp. 40-50). Or demonstrating that no topic-neutral explanation is possible — meta-problem nihilism.

**(e) WHAT WOULD NOT.** Dismissing illusionism as self-refuting — the illusionist accepts cognitive processes, only denies phenomenal properties; addressing only one of the three burdens while neglecting the others; appealing to the prima facie reality of consciousness when the challenge is explaining why that evidence survives scrutiny.

## 1.4 The Epistemic View (Stoljar)

### 1.4.1 The Ignorance Hypothesis

**(a) STEELMAN STATEMENT.** Daniel Stoljar's epistemic view (Stoljar, 2006, 2009) holds that our puzzlement about consciousness stems from ignorance of intrinsic physical properties, not metaphysical reality. Science describes the *structure* of the physical world but not the *intrinsic* or *categorical* nature of entities bearing these relations; these are the consciousness-relevant facts of which we are ignorant (Stoljar, 2006, Ch. 1-3). Stoljar's **Dialectical Thesis** adds that if this is true, the key arguments for consciousness-as-fundamental are unpersuasive. They depend on claims about "all physical facts," but if we are ignorant of relevant facts, either "all physical facts" includes those facts (zombies are not really conceivable) or it does not (the argument shows only that psychology comes apart from *some* physical facts, not all) (Stoljar, 2006, Ch. 4-6). Stoljar's fable illustrates: intelligent slugs detecting triangles and circles but not pie-slices conclude circular facts cannot be determined by triangular facts — a pseudo-problem from ignorance (Stoljar, 2006, pp. 108-110). The pattern generalizes historically: Descartes' argument about language fell to computation; Broad's argument about chemistry fell to quantum chemistry (Stoljar, 2006, pp. 142-155).

**(b) PRIMARY SOURCE(S).** Stoljar, D. (2006). *Ignorance and Imagination: The Epistemic Origin of the Problem of Consciousness*. Oxford University Press. Stoljar, D. (2009). "Physicalism and Panpsychism." *Philosophical Perspectives*, 23, 385-413. NDPR (2007). Review of Stoljar. Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews.

**(c) SEVERITY.** **Major.** The epistemic view deflates the central *motivation* for consciousness-as-fundamental by explaining problem intuitions as products of ignorance. It has not been decisively refuted and is compatible with physical causal closure.

**(d) WHAT WOULD ADDRESS IT.** An argument that no amount of additional physical knowledge could close the gap — that phenomenal concepts (first-person, perspectival, indexical) are such that no third-personal facts could render the absence of phenomenal properties inconceivable.

**(e) WHAT WOULD NOT.** Claiming "we already know all the physical facts" — this begs the question; asserting phenomenal knowledge is "sui generis" without explaining why this entails metaphysical distinctness; dismissing the view as "mysterian" — Stoljar's ignorance is remediable, not permanent.

## 1.5 Critiques of Modal Arguments

### 1.5.1 Block and Stalnaker's Critique of the Conceivability-Possibility Link

**(a) STEELMAN STATEMENT.** The thesis relies on modal arguments — zombies (Chalmers, 1996, 2010), Mary's room (Jackson, 1982, 1986), explanatory gap (Levine, 2001) — inferring from conceivability of physical-mental dissociation to its metaphysical possibility. Block and Stalnaker (1999) argued that a posteriori necessities (Kripke, 1972) show conceivability does not entail possibility in general. They contended standard conceivability arguments depend on "a certain fragile and dubious conception of the relation between conceivability and possibility" (Block & Stalnaker, 1999, pp. 1-5, 15-25). If this holds, the central argumentative route to the thesis is blocked. Type-B physicalists (Papineau, 2002; Block, 2007) reinforce this: physical facts metaphysically necessitate phenomenal facts without a priori entailment — a position conceivability arguments cannot touch.

**(b) PRIMARY SOURCE(S).** Block, N. & Stalnaker, R. (1999). "Conceptual Analysis, Dualism, and the Explanatory Gap." *Philosophical Review*, 108(1), 1-46. Kripke, S. (1972). *Naming and Necessity*. Harvard University Press. Papineau, D. (2002). *Thinking about Consciousness*. Oxford University Press. Yablo, S. (1993). "Is Conceivability a Guide to Possibility?" *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research*, 53(1), 1-42.

**(c) SEVERITY.** **Major.** The modal arguments are the primary *motivation* for the thesis. If the conceivability-possibility inference is unreliable, the central case collapses — though non-modal defenses remain possible.

**(d) WHAT WOULD ADDRESS IT.** A defense of modal rationalism that survives Block-Stalnaker. Chalmers (2002, 2004, 2010) offers the most developed defense.

**(e) WHAT WOULD NOT.** Insisting zombies "seem possible" — Block-Stalnaker showed this is compatible with physicalism given a posteriori necessities; appealing to "intuition" without defending the conceivability-possibility link — precisely what the critiques target.

### 1.5.2 Balog's Zombie Refutation and the Panpsychist Zombie Argument

**(a) STEELMAN STATEMENT.** Katalin Balog (1999) offered a self-undermining argument: if the zombie argument were sound, Zombie-Chalmers in a zombie world would conclude physicalism is false — but physicalism is *true* there by definition. The fault lies in the conceivability-possibility link (Balog, 1999, pp. 5-10). More devastatingly, the very arguments motivating the thesis can be turned against it. Goff (2009, 2017) argued "micro-experiential zombies" — beings whose micro-parts have experience but with no macro-experience — are conceivable. If conceivability entails possibility (the premise against physicalists), the thesis is false. The dilemma: reject the conceivability-possibility inference and undermine the argument against physicalism, or accept the view is refuted by its own methodological commitments (Chalmers, 2016, pp. 195-198; Goff, 2017, pp. 181-186).

**(b) PRIMARY SOURCE(S).** Balog, K. (1999). "Conceivability, Possibility, and the Mind-Body Problem." *Philosophical Review*, 108(4), 497-528. Goff, P. (2009). "Why Panpsychism Doesn't Help Us Explain Consciousness." *Dialectica*, 63(3), 289-311. Goff, P. (2017). *Consciousness and Fundamental Reality*, pp. 181-186. Chalmers, D.J. (2004). "Epistemic Two-Dimensional Semantics." *Philosophical Studies*, 118, 153-226. Cleeveley, H. (2018). "Panpsychism vs the Zombie Argument."

**(c) SEVERITY.** **Major.** Balog undermines the principal argument *for* the thesis; the panpsychist zombie argument threatens internal inconsistency. The thesis depends on tools that, applied consistently, undermine it.

**(d) WHAT WOULD ADDRESS IT.** Rejecting the conceivability-possibility inference uniformly — both against physicalism and panpsychism — and developing a non-modal argumentative foundation. Or demonstrating that micro-experiential zombies are not ideally conceivable.

**(e) WHAT WOULD NOT.** Rejecting modal rationalism only when it threatens panpsychism while accepting it against physicalism — dialectically inconsistent; claiming these arguments "miss the point" — they deploy the very same tools the thesis uses.

**Aggregate Assessment:** Of 8 counter-argument groupings, 4 are rated **Show-stopper** and 4 are rated **Major**. The show-stopper cluster — combination problem (1.1) and relocation/missing-entities (1.2) — strikes at the core mechanism and explanatory promise. The major-rating cluster — illusionism (1.3), epistemic view (1.4), and modal critiques (1.5) — undermines argumentative motivation. Together these constitute a comprehensive adversarial challenge.
