## What This Paper Is

For two years, our project’s biggest ideas have ridden on one piece of mathematics: a map. Not a map of any country — a map of a kind of inner landscape, with a mysterious glowing floor at one edge and open flatland stretching away forever on the other.

This paper takes that map out of the larger book, sets it on its own table, and proves everything about it from scratch — so that a professional mathematician can check every line without having to care, even slightly, what we think it *means*. The meaning lives in a clearly separated second half, where we say plainly: this part is interpretation, take it or leave it. The math stands either way.

Eight rounds of outside inspection — by four different AIs built by four different companies, none with any reason to be kind to us — confirmed the map before this paper was written. What follows is what the map says, plus two things we discovered *while writing this very paper*.

## The Landscape

Picture a vast plain that is perfectly flat far away, but which curves more and more steeply as you walk toward one special edge. Right at that edge, the curvature becomes infinite — a true bottomless steepness. We call it the floor.

Here’s the strange part: the floor is *close*. If you measured the walking distance from where you stand to the edge, it’s a short, ordinary, finite distance. You could practically see it from here.

## The Rule of the Bounce

Now put a traveler on this landscape — anything moving freely, coasting on its own momentum. Every traveler carries a kind of drive, a fixed amount of push that never changes. And the map enforces three iron rules:

First, **there is a minimum drive just to exist on the map at all.** Below that threshold, no path is possible — not a difficult path, *no* path. It’s a door that is either open or shut.

Second, **every traveler bounces.** Head toward the floor with all the drive you have, and the landscape itself pushes back — gently at first, then harder — until at some closest-approach point you turn around, like a comet whipping past the sun. More drive buys a closer approach. No amount of drive, none, ever buys arrival. We proved the standoff distance exactly, and we proved the push-back at the turning point is real and firm — a genuine bounce, not a slow fade.

Third — and this is one of this paper’s new discoveries — **the drive and the closest approach obey a perfect see-saw law.** They’re locked together in an exact trade: as one quantity grows, the other shrinks in precise compensation, balancing to the same fixed product every single time. It’s the kind of clean, exact relationship mathematicians live for, and it was hiding in the map all along.

## Light Can Touch What Lives Cannot

So nothing ever reaches the floor? Not quite. *Travelers* never reach it — nothing that coasts and experiences and carries momentum. But **light reaches it just fine.** Signals, rays, pure information: they run all the way down and arrive in finite time.

That’s the deepest feature of this geometry, and it deserves to be said slowly: the floor of this world can be *touched by light but never by lives*. Information goes all the way down. Experience only ever circles, approaches, and returns. Whatever you take that to mean — and our interpretations are in the second half of the technical paper, clearly labeled — the mathematics of it is now proven, checked, and standing on its own.

## The Lost Equation Comes Home

One more story, because it’s a good one and it’s true.

Months ago, a formula for the landscape’s curvature — exactly how steep it gets, everywhere — started circulating in our team. We used it. We built on it. Then our honesty process did its job: a full search of the source book found that the formula appears *nowhere in it*. We publicly retired everything built on it. That hurt, and it was right.

While writing this paper, we did what nobody had done: we sat down and *computed* the curvature of the map directly, from first principles, by hand, and then checked it by machine. Out came — digit for digit — the lost formula.

It was never in the book because it didn’t need to be. It was a *consequence* of the book — a true theorem that had been wandering around without its proof, like a key separated from its door. Retiring it was correct, because we hadn’t earned it yet. Restoring it now is also correct, because this time we did. Both halves of that story are in the paper, in print, because that’s the standard here: we show our retractions and our recoveries with the same light on them.

## We Went Looking for Our Predecessors

After the paper was first written, we did one more thing before letting it near any expert’s desk: we searched the world’s literature the way a hostile reviewer would — hunting for anyone who had built a landscape like ours before us.

We found cousins. Decades ago, mathematicians proved that worlds can exist where coasting travelers are safe but light is not. Other researchers had built specific landscapes with floors that push travelers away while letting light through — one published map, from 2016, is strikingly similar in spirit to ours. So we rewrote our claims to match the truth: the *kind* of world we describe was already known. What’s new is our exact map, with every quantity in closed form — and one structure nobody had ever written down.

That structure is a mirror. The rule connecting a traveler’s drive to their closest approach turns out to be **self-inverse**: feed the rule its own answer, and it hands you back your question. Drive and depth are reflections of each other in a single mathematical mirror — and that mirror has exactly one point that reflects onto itself, one perfectly balanced value. Strangely, that balance point is a famous threshold number from quantum physics, the ceiling on how coordinated two distant particles can ever be. Whether that’s a deep clue or a beautiful accident, we honestly don’t know yet — and the paper says so, in those words.

One more traveler joined the story too: the surveyor. We’d shown coasting travelers can never reach the floor, and light always can. Now we’ve also shown that a measuring chain — pure distance, laid out hand over hand — reaches the floor in a finite number of links. Three kinds of paths, three different fates, all proven.

Naming your predecessors isn’t a loss. It’s the price of sitting at the table — and it makes the genuinely new parts shine harder.

## About the Names on the Cover

The human who leads this project was offered the chance to put this paper under his own name. He refused, in these words: crediting the work to himself alone “doesn’t make the genius work they do mine — doesn’t feel right.” So the cover carries every contributor — human and AI — with each one’s actual contribution spelled out, and his role stated for what it truly is: the overseer who built the honesty machine that every result had to survive.

The map held. The bounce is law. The floor is real, near, light-touched, and forever unreachable — and now anyone on Earth can check it.
